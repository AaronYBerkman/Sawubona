              Letters worth a second look

              • T — fold your index finger down over the tip of your thumb. The American T, with the thumb poking out between index and middle finger, is not the South African one; every SASL presenter this learned from uses the folded index.
              • Y — thumb and little finger out, as in ASL.
              • U, V, R — two fingers up: together, apart, or crossed.
              • G and H — pointing sideways with one finger, or with two.
              • P and Q — the K hand and the G hand, turned to point down.
              • J and Z are drawn in the air: the I hand draws a J, the index finger a Z. The camera reads them from the fingertip's path, not a held shape, so draw them clearly and not too fast.

                How it works

                What this does

                Your camera feed goes through hand, body and face tracking on this device. A recorded sign becomes a sequence of points, and two families of pretrained sign models describe it in a way that ignores who is signing. That description is compared with 3,483 reference clips of 1,858 signs from the Real SASL dictionary and the National Institute for the Deaf's SASL Video Dictionary. Nothing is uploaded, and there is nothing to set up: it was built to work for someone it has never seen.

                How well it works

                Measured on signers the models had never seen, never on the data they learned from:

                • 96%Fingerspelling. Held letters read correctly, tested on each of five SASL presenters in turn after training on the other four.
                • 82%A 10-word lesson. The top answer is right 82% of the time, and the right word is in the top three 96% of the time.
                • 3 in 10The whole dictionary. That is why practice happens in lessons: the smaller the list, the more reliable the answer.
                • 82%The quiz. Sign the word asked and it says “matched” 82% of the time and “close” 15%; sign an unrelated word and it wrongly says “matched” about one time in twenty.

                Limits

                What it does not do

                • It is not a translator. No system can yet translate signed language from a webcam, and this one does not try.
                • A match is not a grade. “That looked like FRIDAY” is not the same as “that was a perfect FRIDAY”. Your teacher is the judge of that.
                • Facial grammar is not judged. Facial expression, eyebrows and the use of space carry SASL grammar. The face is tracked as part of a sign’s shape, but none of that grammar is interpreted.
                • SASL varies. Signs differ between regions and schools. If your course teaches a different version from Real SASL, trust your course.

                Where it comes from

                • Reference signs: Real SASL. The app keeps only numbers derived from the clips (the tracked movement of the body, hands and face outline) and links to the site for the videos.
                • Letters learned from SASL alphabet videos by the National Institute for the Deaf, The Sign Tutors, and three other presenters, every example checked by hand; and from ASL-HG (Mendeley Data, CC BY 4.0) for the letters SASL shares with ASL.
                • Sign models: SignCLIP (Jiang et al., 2024) and OpenHands (AI4Bharat). Tracking: MediaPipe.
                • Drawings: made for this app — the letter hands are traced from real, hand-checked frames; the plants and mountains are drawn by code. The drawn signer is made from the points tracked in each Real SASL clip: an illustration of the movement, not the video, so it always links the original.
